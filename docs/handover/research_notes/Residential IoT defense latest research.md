# Measured outcomes now outrank simulators in smart-home defense

**Executive summary.** Across 2023 to October 2026, the research most relevant to a residential IoT home-gateway defender converges on one message: results measured against real or emulated outcomes are trusted, while results scored by simulator-assumed rewards, in-sample accuracy, or fixed scripted attackers are increasingly distrusted. Reinforcement-learning (RL) defenders trained in simulation can score below a do-nothing agent once realistic benign noise is added ([Nyberg et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16541)) and can be broken by learned or LLM attackers ([Trident 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04317)). Intrusion-detection (IDS) accuracy of 98 to 100 percent within one dataset collapses to near-chance or to mean leave-one-dataset-out F1 of 0.39 to 0.47 when tested elsewhere ([Cantone et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10974); [BRIDGE 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11324)). Stackelberg and deception papers mostly use analytically defined or simulated payoffs, and a deception layer can be defeated by a reasoning attacker ([Rouxii 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26555)). In real homes, the threat is dominated by old CVEs, default credentials, preinstalled malware and proxy-relayed LAN infection, and independent testing found commercial home safeguards detected only about 3 of 14 threat types ([Mandalari et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03045v1)). The iot-defense project (as described in its own records) sits squarely in this critique: a 693-row measured outcome table used to grade rule-based, Stackelberg and PPO policies by regret is, in this limited literature search, a gap-filler, because no public table of measured defender-action-versus-attack outcomes with collateral damage was found for IoT. Its honest negative findings (no "Stackelberg is best" claim, circular PPO, benign-traffic false alarms, misnamed beacons) match the field's current self-criticism. The main risks are that the lab detector numbers are in-sample, that benign false-alarm rates (17 to 21 firewall actions per hour per Echo) would swamp a real home, that long-horizon beacons need a different feature than a 60-second window, and that novelty claims rest on a snippet-level search. Many items below are snippet-only or unverified; the confidence section lists what to re-check before citing.

## The threat is old exploits, proxies and preinstalled malware, not exotic attacks

Residential devices are now the raw material of record-size DDoS botnets and residential-proxy businesses. Cloudflare reported a **29.7 Tbps** attack in its Q3 2025 report, with the Aisuru botnet estimated at 1 to 4 million infected hosts ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/ddos-threat-report-2025-q3/)); later coverage reports a **31.4 Tbps** attack lasting 35 seconds from Aisuru/Kimwolf ([BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/aisuru-botnet-sets-new-record-with-314-tbps-ddos-attack)), though sources disagree on whether it occurred in November or December 2025. Kimwolf is an Android botnet of roughly 2 million devices (an earlier count was about 1.8 million), mostly unofficial Android TV boxes entered through an exposed ADB service, and it spreads by abusing residential-proxy providers that let requests reach the proxy user's local network, which means it reaches devices behind home NAT ([Krebs](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/01/who-operates-the-badbox-2-0-botnet/); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/01/kimwolf-android-botnet-infects-over-2.html)). The FBI warned in June 2025 that TV streaming boxes, projectors, car infotainment units and picture frames, mostly China-made, are compromised before purchase or at setup and joined to the BADBOX 2.0 botnet and proxy services ([FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250605)). Botnet size claims are unstable: BADBOX figures range from over 1 million (HUMAN Security's original research) to over 10 million (Google's lawsuit as summarised by Krebs), and Eleven11bot was first sized at about 30,000 devices, then 86,000 by Shadowserver, then argued by GreyNoise to be far smaller ([Krebs](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/01/who-operates-the-badbox-2-0-botnet/); [Cybersecurity Dive](https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/eleven11bot-revised-downward-mirai/741923/)). The honest reading is a range with a date, not a single figure.

Home-focused telemetry says the exploitation is mundane. A Bitdefender and NETGEAR report covering more than 6.1 million smart homes found an average of 22 connected devices and about **29 attacks per day per home**, and **99.4 percent** of IoT exploits targeted known, already-fixed CVEs ([Bitdefender](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/news/new-bitdefender-and-netgear-report-reveals-rising-threats-across-the-connected-home-landscape)); this is vendor telemetry from vendors who sell home security, so it carries commercial framing. Streaming devices (25.9 percent) and smart TVs (21.3 percent) led the detected vulnerabilities in that report. This sits in tension with the independent evidence on defenses: the Mandalari et al. preprint ran more than 4,000 automated attacks against 79 devices and eight commercial safeguards, found an average of only about 3 of 14 threat types detected, found that threats originating from IoT devices went undetected, and found that seven of eight products depend on cloud processing ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03045v1)). It is a 2023 preprint with 2023-era products and does not cover eero Secure or Netgear Armor, so it supports "claims were untested" more than "products are worthless". India-specific evidence is thin and partly low-quality: a police-reported case involving at least 50,000 CCTV cameras came through a snippet-level source, while the March 2026 Ghaziabad CCTV case led to a nationwide audit order ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/03/26/india_pakistan_cctv/); [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/04/223-india-bans-chinese-cctv-makers-april-2026/)).

Home-network defense research is moving toward lightweight, gateway-resident enforcement. Feraudo et al. extend MUD (RFC 8520) with rate limits and enforce them at a home gateway through eBPF and iptables ([arXiv 2305.02186](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02186)); MUD adoption is blocked by the absence of vendor-supplied MUD files ([RIPE 85](https://ripe85.ripe.net/presentations/106-RIPE-85_OpenWrt_MUD.pdf)). Measurements show that LAN-local traffic is itself a leak and attack surface ([IMC 2023 local-communication study](https://moniotrlab.khoury.northeastern.edu/publications/iot-local-imc23/)), and Matter has design-level denial-of-service flaws across Wi-Fi, BLE and Thread ([MaDoS, EURECOM](https://www.eurecom.edu/fr/publication/8773)). An IP-layer gateway cannot see most Thread or BLE traffic, so radio-layer attacks belong in the threat model as out of scope. IoT-23 captures date from 2018 to 2019 and predate proxyware, ADB-driven infection and ad-fraud traffic; the project should call it a historical proxy.

### Regulation binds manufacturers; data-protection law binds the defender

Regulation is converging on secure-by-design duties for manufacturers rather than on what a home defender may do. The EU Cyber Resilience Act's reporting duties apply from 11 September 2026 and its main obligations from 11 December 2027 ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cra-reporting)); the UK PSTI regime has applied since 29 April 2024 ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/regulations-consumer-connectable-product-security)); the US Cyber Trust Mark lost its first lead administrator in December 2025 and the FCC selected the ioXt Alliance on 13 April 2026 ([Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2026/04/fcc-selects-ioxt-alliance-lead-cyber-labeling-program/412800)). India's CCTV rules require STQC testing, with trade sources disagreeing on whether the effective date was April 2025 or 1 April 2026 ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/04/223-india-bans-chinese-cctv-makers-april-2026/)), and the DPDP Rules notified in November 2025 phase in notice, consent and safeguard duties around May 2027 ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf)); all of these dates came from secondary or snippet sources. The notes found no regulation or legal analysis of active response (blocking, decoy redirection) by consumer gateways, and that absence is a "not found" rather than "none exists". The inference, which is not legal advice, is that a gateway storing payloads or per-device logs processes household members' personal data, so forensic capture should default to time-limited, local flow metadata with opt-in payload capture.

Human-factors evidence on automated blocking is sparse. A 2025 IMWUT study of automation levels in smart-home warnings (48 participants) found higher automation lowered the sense of interruption but that customisation is essential, though it concerns physical hazards ([PEASEC](https://www.peasec.de/paper/2025/2025_HenkelHaeslerAlNajmiHesselReuter_HouseThatSavesMe_IMWUT.pdf)); an online study with 130 participants found intermediate-length LLM-written alert notifications worked best ([arXiv 2510.21508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21508)). No study of homeowner tolerance for automated device blocking, decoys or forensic capture was found.

## Game-theory and deception papers rarely ground payoffs in measurement

The Stackelberg and deception literature since 2023 mostly defines payoffs analytically or in simulation. Park and Dagher's Bayesian Stackelberg honeypot allocation scales to 500 nodes but the abstract does not say how payoffs or type priors are set or whether evaluation is simulated ([arXiv 2505.16043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16043)); Albaseer et al.'s Stackelberg honeypot paper (ICNC 2024) was seen only as an abstract record whose description reads oddly for a security paper ([HBKU](https://elmi.hbku.edu.qa/en/publications/adaptive-honeypot-defense-deployment-a-stackelberg-game-approach-/)). The closest measurement-grounded pipeline is Hammar and Stadler's: collect measurements in an emulation, identify a game model, optimise in simulation, evaluate back in emulation ([thesis, arXiv 2502.02541](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02541); [CSLE, arXiv 2604.15590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15590)), though their formulation is optimal stopping and Markov games rather than Stackelberg, and their action set targets enterprise-like infrastructure. Their conjectural online learning drops the assumption that the game model is correctly specified ([IEEE TIFS 2025, arXiv 2402.12499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12499)), which is the nearest published relative of hand-set priors that may be wrong.

The long-standing criticisms are rational-attacker assumptions and payoff realism. Quantal-response and prospect-theory models fit human adversaries better than perfect rationality in physical-security games ([Yang et al., IJCAI 2011](https://www.ijcai.org/Abstract/11/084)), and inverse-game-theory work infers follower payoffs from behaviour ([Wu et al., NeurIPS 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01380)). A 2025 industrial-IoT deep-RL deception paper admits that attacker responses are assumed, attackers are non-adaptive, telemetry is simulated and there is no field deployment, and it reports only **13.75 percent** attack mitigation, an odd headline that the notes flag for rechecking ([D3O-IIoT, Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12816736/)). Deception itself is fragile: HoneyIoT used RL on a real Internet-facing device and claimed to stay covert against common honeypot-detection tools ([WiSec 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06430)), while the September 2026 Rouxii paper reports that a counter-deception prompt raised an LLM pentester's correct honeypot identification from **19 percent to 97 percent** ([arXiv 2609.26555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26555)). These two sources do not contradict each other (different attackers, different years) but they bound the claim: decoy value is conditional on attacker knowledge. Related LLM-attacker deception papers (VelLMes, AgentSnare, Honeyquest) were snippet-only and two have unusual identifiers, so they are not relied on here.

## RL defenders look strong in simulation and weak under realistic opposition

Autonomous cyber defense (CAGE challenges, CybORG, Cyberwheel, Yawning Titan) is dominated by simulator-trained PPO, hierarchical and graph agents scored against scripted attackers. The 2026 critique is direct. Hicks et al. split the sim-to-real gap into a virtualisation gap and a modelling gap and recommend against fixed red strategies for final evaluation, grounding benign traffic in real logs, masking invalid actions, checking rewards for hacking, and reporting multiple seeds with confidence intervals ([arXiv 2604.08805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08805)). Nyberg et al. trained a PPO graph agent in a simulator and tested it in a 37-VM cyber range over 134 two-hour episodes: against the guided attacker, mean return was -2883 for doing nothing, -9525 for a heuristic, and -13205 for the plain learned agent, and simulated benign users drove most of the drop through false alerts that triggered needless defensive actions; training with 1 percent noise improved the learned agent to -5812 ([arXiv 2609.16541](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16541)). Trident reports that an LLM red-team agent cut blue performance by an average 522 percent against live DRL defenders in CAGE-4 and Cyberwheel, a figure over 100 percent that should be checked against the paper's metric definition ([arXiv 2608.04317](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04317)).

LLM defenders do not fix this. In CAGE-4, an all-LLM team (GPT-4o-mini) scored -2547 against -493 for an all-RL team, and was worse than no defenders against one attacker, while being about 104 times slower per episode (a different, unreconciled figure of about 150 times appears in the same paper) ([Castro et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04843)). OpenSec found GPT-5.2 contained in 100 percent of 40 episodes with an 82.5 percent false-positive rate, concluding that the calibration gap is restraint rather than detection ([arXiv 2601.21083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21083)). Offline-RL and OPE literature adds that learned policies often fail to beat the behaviour policy and that OPE rarely helped ([NeoRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00714)). For statistics, the standard references are stratified-bootstrap reporting with IQM and probability of improvement ([Agarwal et al., NeurIPS 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264)) and the caution that bootstrap intervals under-cover at about 10 episodes ([arXiv 2102.03607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.03607)). No cyber-defense paper was found that applies formal shielding to containment actions, that studies circular train-and-test on one measured table, or that compares Stackelberg leader policies against PPO for response selection.

## Detection accuracy collapses outside its dataset, and benign home traffic is under-measured

The workhorse IoT datasets (IoT-23, CICIoT2023, CICIoMT2024, Edge-IIoTset, TON_IoT, Bot-IoT, N-BaIoT) are lab or testbed captures with scripted benign traffic, rule-derived labels and heavy imbalance. IoT-23's own authors argue most security datasets lack comprehensive labelling criteria and propose a labelling ontology ([arXiv 2305.01337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01337)); IoT-23's command-and-control (C&C) labels are heuristic, based on response-byte thresholds and known C&C endpoints, with some classes having only a handful of flows ([Stratosphere IoT-23](https://www.stratosphereips.org/datasets-iot23)), though the flow counts came from an unnamed study and no paper auditing IoT-23 C&C labels was found. Flow-exporter choice changes feature relevance across Bot-IoT, IoT-23 and CICIoT23 ([Vitorino et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08345)), and even stream ordering under a fixed split can reverse scorer rankings ([arXiv 2605.24696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24696)). Generalisation collapse is documented repeatedly: near-chance cross-dataset accuracy ([Cantone et al., IEEE Access 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10974)); mean leave-one-dataset-out F1 of 0.39 to 0.47 across five datasets, where the authors' own method reaches 0.8296 ([BRIDGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11324)); reliance on coarse port features that appear 96 to 435 times more often in source-domain attacks, with adversarial robustness unrelated to cross-network generalisation ([Hakim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00553)); and a drop from about 0.98 F1 on a static split to normalised area-under-time of 0.28 to 0.57 under temporal forward-chaining ([SBSeg 2026](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbseg/article/view/44315), garbled extraction, verify). The canonical pitfall checklist is Arp et al. ([USENIX Security 2022](https://usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/arp)), and Apruzzese et al. add that papers ignore deployment realities ([EuroS&P 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00550)).

On false alarms, the evidence supports accumulation and per-device baselines but is mixed on the second. CUMAD cut false positives on N-BaIoT from about 3.57 percent to about 0.5 percent with a sequential probability ratio test, detecting compromised devices in under five observations on average ([arXiv 2404.13690](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13690)). A per-device autoencoder study on CICIoMT2024 cameras found per-feature thresholds can combine into a higher aggregate false-positive rate and that specialisation does not always win ([SBSeg](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbseg/article/download/44335/44098/), snippet only); an older user-behaviour model struggled when a new device was added. No paper was found that reports alerts per device-hour on real benign smart-home traffic for a modern detector. For C&C, periodicity across multi-flow time series is the best-supported signal: Oskera et al. apply the Lomb-Scargle periodogram and stress that capture duration drives recall ([CNSM 2025](https://dl.ifip.org/db/conf/cnsm/cnsm2025/1571190192.pdf)), and window-size studies explore 5 to 30 seconds without a smart-home specific result ([FLARE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15375) snippet). LLM and foundation-model detectors match but do not beat random forest on known attacks (F1 0.7124 versus 0.7159) and reach 42.63 percent accuracy on unseen attack types in the one fetched study ([arXiv 2601.15269](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15269)). The freshest home-like dataset, SHARP (2025), warns that no telemetry observation carries a verified positive attack-effect label and requires an IEEE DataPort subscription ([IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/sharp-smart-home-attacks-across-residential-multi-protocols)).

## Response effectiveness is rarely measured, and collateral damage almost never

The central finding for the project is a gap. Response evaluation is dominated by simulated RL environments with designer-assumed rewards (CybORG/CAGE, [Cyberwheel](https://www.ornl.gov/publication/towards-high-fidelity-training-environment-autonomous-cyber-defense-agents)) and by single-system demos reporting latency or percent of attack traffic dropped. Examples include a middleware with F1 0.931 and containment within 300 ms ([Frontiers in AI 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12753915/), abstract only), an access-control loop with 335.8 ms eviction on one testbed device and no collateral analysis ([arXiv 2607.11649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11649)), and Raspberry Pi eBPF/XDP mitigation claiming over 97 percent effectiveness against a 100 Mbps flood with legitimate traffic "unaffected" stated qualitatively ([Tolay, arXiv 2508.00851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00851)). A separate non-peer-reviewed preprint reports about 94 percent mitigation and over 90 percent legitimate throughput retained ([Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10908211)), which is the only retention figure found. Tolay's June 2026 follow-up proposes reversible, time-limited actions with critical-device safeguards but explicitly reports no new measurements ([arXiv 2606.10508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10508)). The closest treatment of quarantine collateral frames it as detection rate versus legitimate devices quarantined, a detector trade-off rather than a per-action cost ([snippet review](https://www.themoonlight.io/es/review/quarantining-malicious-iot-devices-in-intelligent-sliced-mobile-networks)).

The nearest tooling competitor is MininetGym, a Gymnasium wrapper for RL agents in Mininet security scenarios (AAMAS 2026 demo track) ([PDF](https://ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2026/pdfs/VVUY3381.pdf)): same substrate, different philosophy, since reward there is designer-defined. VetIoT builds a virtual testbed per defense but targets policy enforcement ([CNS 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12417)). Mininet fidelity evidence is throughput-only, with sharing error under 9 percent for 1 to 20 nodes and trouble with high-bandwidth links ([ICUMT 2025](https://phd.dii.unipi.it/pubblicazioni/item/7270-l-borgianni,-c-bua,-e-coli,-d-adami,-s-giordano-“on-the-accuracy-of-mininet-comparing-net-work-emulation-and-physical-testbeds-for-distributed-systems”,-17th-international-congress-on-ul-tra-modern-telecommunication-and-control-systems-2025.html)), so no source quantifies device, firmware or radio sim-to-real gaps for IoT. Cost-sensitive IDS evaluation is mature but old (minimum expected cost, intrusion detection capability), and recent work still argues F1 and false-positive rate do not express operational cost ([Mitrokotsa et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0807.2043); [WOCI, SBSeg](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbseg/article/download/44362/44125/)); the link from these to response-action cost is thin. Rigor guidance is strong but mostly not adopted: Arp et al.; the NDSS 2026 "Chasing Shadows" finding that all 72 reviewed LLM-security papers had at least one pitfall ([NDSS](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/chasing-shadows-pitfalls-in-llm-security-research/)); and arguments that preregistration certifies pre-hoc evaluation choices, with the caveat that authors often deviate from plans ([arXiv 2407.12220](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12220); the deviation claim's source was not captured). No security-venue registered-report track was found. For unseen-attack priors, expert-elicitation methods (Cooke, IDEA/Delphi) usually lack ground truth, and a 2026 workshop report says calibrating LLM-simulated experts against ground truth is unresolved ([arXiv 2609.03178](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03178)); no work was found that scores blind priors for unseen attacks against measured outcomes.

Three cross-cutting judgements follow. First, the project is strongest when presented as a measured benchmark and weakest if read as a detector paper; the literature's most severe critiques (in-sample accuracy, IoT-23 age, no home deployment) fall on the detector, not the benchmark. Second, the apparent conflict between the table-regret result for Stackelberg and the live tie is a feature to explain, not hide: Nyberg et al. show that conclusions differ across simulation and range, and the notes recommend reporting a permutation or exact paired test alongside bootstrap intervals because OPE bootstrap intervals under-cover at small K. Third, the project's "per-device baselines" plan is supported by CUMAD but contested by the per-device CICIoMT2024 camera study, so it should be evaluated rather than assumed.

## Confidence and verification

Overall confidence is moderate for the direction of the literature (several independent 2024 to 2026 sources agree on simulator brittleness, cross-dataset collapse and rare collateral reporting) and low for any individual number or any claim of novelty. The notes were produced from web searches (about 40 tool calls for the RL area), many result pages were snippets, the search tool is described as US-only and weak on recent months, and the five research areas were compiled separately, so cross-area overlap (for example Tolay, Hammar, Hicks) was checked only where they overlapped. "Not found" throughout means not found, not absent.

Items that need re-checking before citation, as flagged by the researchers: several 2026 arXiv identifiers (2604, 2606 to 2609 series) post-date the researchers' training data and only some were opened, so every 26xx ID should be confirmed to resolve to the stated title and authors; the notes specifically flag Honeyquest (2606.21037, known only through a vendor whitepaper), AgentSnare (2607.26998), 2608.17650, 2606.21422, 2606.21513 and 2609.06012 as snippet-only. Odd metrics to verify against metric definitions: Trident's 522 percent blue-performance reduction, IRCopilot's 114 to 150 percent sub-task completion (not quoted here), D3O-IIoT's 13.75 percent mitigation, the CICIoT2023 "54.8 percent duplicates" figure (not confirmed on the opened abstract and therefore not used), the "Bilal et al." 30-point federated macro-F1 loss (second-hand), Flood et al. and Nougnanke et al. cross-dataset gains, and the Korba et al. detection and delay figures. Authorship and venue items from memory or inferred: Arp et al.'s author list, Kitsune, N-BaIoT, DIoT and Axelsson, Kostrikov and Nachum, the IMWUT DOI for Henkel et al., the lead author of the IMC 2023 local-communication study, the CAGE-4 fifth-place claim for Kiely et al., and Park and Dagher and Albaseer payoff construction (only abstracts read). The Albaseer abstract description reads oddly for a security paper and should be treated as unverified.

Conflicting claims left unresolved: the 31.4 Tbps attack date (November versus December 2025); BADBOX scale (1 million versus over 10 million) and Eleven11bot scale (about 30,000, 86,000, and a much lower later estimate); Kimwolf at 1.8 versus about 2 million; the India CCTV effective date (April 2025 versus 1 April 2026) and BIS involvement; the DPDP Rules notification day (13 versus 14 November 2025); Castro et al.'s 104x versus about 150x speed ratio; Tolay's abstract versus snippet figures (the abstract says over 97 percent mitigation under 100 Mbps; a snippet gave about 97 percent of about 30k pps and about 85 percent of one core, unverified) alongside the Research Square 94 percent figure; per-device baselines helping (CUMAD, N-BaIoT lineage) versus not always winning (SBSeg CICIoMT2024); and the Echo rate (17 to 28 per hour in the notes versus 17 to 21 from the project brief). The Cloudflare H1 2026 figures came from a low-quality aggregator and the primary page was not opened. Legal and regulatory dates (CRA, RED, PSTI, Cyber Trust Mark, India CCTV, DPDP) mostly came from secondary or snippet sources and the primary texts (EUR-Lex, ETSI EN 303 645, FCC DA 26-75, the Indian gazette notifications) were not retrieved; a TechRadar "4 Jan 2027" federal-procurement deadline is explicitly unverified and not used. The SDG, sustainability and responsible-use section was not researched in depth: no sources were retrieved, and the SDG 9/11/16 mapping and the responsible-use points in the notes are suggestions, not cited findings. Finally, the effect of Mininet fidelity, the age of IoT-23, and the absence of a household deployment or user study are acknowledged limitations on the project side, taken from the project framing and the notes, not from new evidence.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what counts as a defensible claim in this area. A policy that wins in a simulator, a detector that scores 99 percent in-sample, a Stackelberg model with unexplained payoffs, and an LLM that explains its containment all share the same weakness: they were never graded against measured outcomes under realistic benign noise and a reacting attacker. iot-defense's most defensible position is therefore methodological. It offers a measured, reusable table plus an honest account of where each policy family fails, and it does not need Stackelberg to win. The project's own negative results (a live tie, a circular PPO, benign false alarms, misnamed beacons) line up with 2026 findings from independent groups, which makes them credible rather than embarrassing.

The decisive open question is whether the table generalises beyond Mininet and beyond the attacks that populated it. The pre-registered blind-prior experiment and the real-hardware replication test exactly that, and the literature offers no precedent that would let a reviewer dismiss either as redundant. Two risks deserve early attention because they can invalidate downstream results: the benign false-alarm rate, which determines whether a gateway would be switched off by its owner, and the unverified novelty claim, which should be checked against dataset repositories and full texts of the closest papers before the paper rewrite commits to it.
